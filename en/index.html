Which records you can find

For each record type we open the search in archive collections of the U.S., Canada, Latin America, Europe, Israel, South Africa and Australia — with your details filled in. We store nothing.

Town & community

Where your town's records are held today, whether there is a memorial book, a community website, lists of residents. Start here if you know the place.

Ship passenger lists

Age, hometown, occupation, the relative left behind and whom they were joining in the new country. Often the only document naming the shtetl.

Naturalization & citizenship

Declaration of intention and petition: date and place of birth, ship and arrival date, names of spouse and children, sometimes a photo.

Birth, marriage, death

Metrical books and certificates: exact dates, parents' names (incl. mother's maiden name), residence, witnesses.

Censuses

The whole household at one address: ages, year of arrival, native language, citizenship, occupation. Censuses of the U.S., Canada, England, Argentina and Mexico are free.

Military records

U.S. draft cards (address, birth date and place, nearest relative) and Soviet WWII soldiers' records: awards, losses, hospitals.

Holocaust & deportations

Pages of Testimony, deportation lists, camp and displaced-persons records — often with parents' names and pre-war residence.

Burials

Gravestones with dates; Jewish gravestones usually give the father's name — the key to the next generation.

Soviet repression

Arrest date, sentence, place of exile or death, rehabilitation.

How to find your ancestors: a step-by-step guide

Finding ancestors by surname is a small investigation, not a single click. Here is a proven order of steps for families from the Russian Empire, the USSR and Eastern Europe — and for anyone whose relatives emigrated to the United States, Israel or elsewhere.

1

Start with family and home documents

Write down everything older relatives remember: full names, patronymics, maiden names, years and places of birth, moves. Gather birth and marriage certificates, military papers, letters and photos with notes on the back — they are the keys to archival records.

2

Check every spelling of the surname

Records spelled names differently: Goldberg, Holdberg, Goldburg; Ivanov, Iwanow, Ivanoff. Our search transliterates Cyrillic automatically and suggests alternative spellings. First names changed too: Sheindel might become Sheina, Jennie or Jane.

3

Search for records, not just a surname

One person leaves traces in many records: a ship manifest, a naturalization petition, a birth record, a census, a draft card, a gravestone. Enter the surname, years, home town and the country the family moved to — Istoki opens the search directly in the right archive collections of the U.S., Canada, Argentina, Brazil, England, Israel and Eastern Europe.

4

Save your findings and build a tree

Export results to CSV, keep links to the sources and note who is related to whom. For a family chronicle, tree and memory book, try Mystorytel.

Where to look: by country and topic

Ancestors who emigrated to the United States

Ellis Island passenger lists (1892–1924), U.S. censuses 1900–1950, naturalization and draft records at the National Archives (NARA), and obituaries and notices in historic American newspapers. Find a Grave and BillionGraves often show gravestones with dates and parents' names.

Russian Empire: Ukraine, Belarus, Moldova, Lithuania, Poland

The core sources are metrical books (births, marriages, deaths), revision lists and the 1897 census. Many records are digitized and indexed by JewishGen, JRI-Poland, FamilySearch and regional archives. For Bessarabia and Podolia, Jewish metrical books and voter lists are especially useful.

Soviet soldiers of World War II

Information on soldiers, awards and burial places is collected in Pamyat Naroda, OBD Memorial and Podvig Naroda. Search by surname, first name and birth year — and try different spellings of the patronymic.

Holocaust victims and victims of repression

Yad Vashem's Central Database of Shoah Victims' Names, the Arolsen Archives (displaced persons and camp records) and the USHMM database. For victims of Soviet political repression — the Open List project.

Jewish genealogy

The National Library of Israel holds newspapers, community memorial (Yizkor) books, photographs and archives in Yiddish, Hebrew and Russian. Yizkor books often list residents of a shtetl and their fates — search by surname and town name.

Europe and Israel

Vital records of the Netherlands, Belgium and France are in Open Archives; French newspapers, including the Russian émigré press, are in Gallica; European museums and archives are in Europeana; Israeli archives and press are in the National Library of Israel.

Frequently asked questions

Is the ancestor search free?

Yes — completely free, no sign-up. We only search open databases, and every result opens on the source's own website.

How do I find relatives by surname?

Enter a surname (Cyrillic works too) and, if you know them, a first name, birth years and a place. Istoki searches family trees, archival records, newspapers and photo collections at once. If there's no exact first-name match, it automatically shows everyone with that surname and suggests alternative spellings.

How can I find ancestors who emigrated to America?

Search the surname in different Latin spellings — U.S. clerks often wrote names by ear. Ellis Island passenger lists, U.S. censuses 1900–1950, naturalization records at the National Archives and historic American newspapers are the most useful; links to them appear next to your search results.

Where can I find metrical books of the Russian Empire?

Many are digitized and indexed on FamilySearch, JewishGen and JRI-Poland, and in regional archives of Ukraine, Belarus, Moldova, Lithuania and Latvia. Links are in the “Also search here” panel after you search.

How do I find information about a grandfather who fought in WWII?

Use Pamyat Naroda, OBD Memorial and Podvig Naroda — they hold draft records, awards, wounds and burial places of Soviet soldiers. Search by surname, first name and birth year.

How do I find relatives who died in the Holocaust?

Check Yad Vashem's Central Database of Shoah Victims' Names, the Arolsen Archives and the USHMM database. Also search for your family's town in Yizkor memorial books — many are in the National Library of Israel and the Internet Archive.

Why are results in Latin letters?

Most of the world's archives keep records in Latin script. We transliterate Russian surnames automatically and also search the original spelling where possible, such as Wikipedia and the National Library of Israel.

Do you store my searches or data?

No. Searches run right in your browser, with no sign-up. We store neither queries nor results. Anonymous visit statistics are collected with a web analytics service — the names you enter are never sent to it.

How can I save my findings and build a family tree?

Export results to CSV or copy links to what you found. To build a family tree, collect memories and photos, and print a family memory book, use Mystorytel.
